\section{Results\label{sec:results}}
This section reports the results for semi-supervised code-to-architecture mapping across nine subject systems. RQ1 compares lexical, structural, and contextual file representations using a feature-only classifier. RQ2 examines how mapping performance changes when these representations initialize HGT with file, member, and folder context, and analyzes errors relative to directory-based predictions. RQ3 uses controlled experiments to examine how graph representation, training schedule, and neighbor processing affect the contribution of structural context. For RQ1 and RQ2, we report median macro- and micro-F1 scores over 100 runs per system and configuration. Every test file is scored, regardless of prediction confidence.

Each experimental setting is repeated 100 times, with the same randomly sampled initial labeled set used across compared configurations within each run. For each system and metric, we use the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon}, pairing runs by their shared split. We apply Holm's correction within each comparison family, separately for macro-F1 and micro-F1, at $\alpha=0.05$. We report differences in median performance in F1 percentage points and Cliff's $\delta$~\cite{cliff}, classified by magnitude as negligible (N, $|\delta|<0.147$), small (S, $0.147\leq|\delta|<0.33$), medium (M, $0.33\leq|\delta|<0.474$), or large (L, $|\delta|\geq0.474$).

\subsection*{RQ1. Comparing Lexical, Structural, and Contextual File Representations\label{sec:rq1}}

RQ1 examines how much predictive information each file representation provides before additional graph-based message passing. Table~\ref{tab:rq1_mlp_features} reports the median macro-F1 and micro-F1 scores obtained with lexical W2V, structural N2V, and UniXcoder variants: \zs{} and \ft{}. Each representation is evaluated using the graph-free MLP. This classifier performs no message passing, although N2V encodes dependency topology directly and \ft{} incorporates dependency information through its fine-tuning procedure. Table~\ref{tab:rq1_stats} summarizes the pairwise differences across all ten systems, with the number of systems favoring each method and the number of Holm-significant differences in each direction.

Since the subject systems contain small and imbalanced architectural modules, macro-F1 is generally lower and more variable than micro-F1. N2V achieves the highest median macro-F1 on six systems, \ft{} on SH-3D, T.Mates, and HDC, and W2V on JabRef. For micro-F1, N2V leads on seven systems and \ft{} on SH-3D and T.Mates. On JabRef, W2V and \ft{} do not differ significantly, and both significantly outperform N2V on micro-F1.

Fine-tuning significantly improves UniXcoder over \zs{} on micro-F1 for all ten systems and on macro-F1 for nine, predominantly with large effects. The exception is SH-3D, where the macro-F1 difference is small and not significant; its micro-F1 difference has a medium effect. On JabRef, the macro-F1 effect is medium.

Both dependency-informed representations are generally stronger than the lexical representation. N2V significantly outperforms W2V on both metrics for nine systems, with JabRef as the exception, where W2V is significantly stronger. Similarly, \ft{} significantly outperforms W2V on micro-F1 for nine systems and on macro-F1 for eight. W2V is significantly stronger than \ft{} on JabRef macro-F1, while their micro-F1 difference is not significant; the macro-F1 difference on Lucene is also not significant.

N2V is significantly stronger than \ft{} on both metrics for A.UML, C.Img, Lucene, Bash, HDF, and Chrome, whereas \ft{} is significantly stronger on SH-3D and T.Mates; these differences have large effects. On HDC, the two representations do not differ significantly on either metric. On JabRef, the macro-F1 difference is not significant, while \ft{} achieves significantly higher micro-F1.

\begin{GrayBox}{Answer to RQ1.}
Dependency-informed representations provide the strongest mapping performance on most systems using graph-free MLP. N2V performs best overall, while dependency-guided fine-tuning consistently improves UniXcoder over its zero-shot representation and produces the best macro-F1 on three systems. W2V is weaker on most systems but remains the strongest representation on JabRef. Thus, no single file representation performs best across all systems and metrics.
\end{GrayBox}

\subsection*{RQ2. Mapping Performance as File, Member, and Folder Context Are Added \label{sec:rq2}}
RQ2 examines how mapping performance changes when graph structure is added to each representation. Figure~\ref{fig:rq2_lineplot} reports median macro-F1 and micro-F1 for the graph-free MLP and HGT under F, FM, FF, and FFM. The UniXcoder configurations use the fine-tuned variant, \ft{}. F adds message passing over file dependencies, FM additionally introduces member nodes, and FF and FFM introduce folder nodes. These configurations also differ in how member information enters the model. W2V includes member tokens in the file vector for MLP, F, and FF, but uses separately trained file and member representations for FM and FFM. In contrast, \ft{} does not aggregate member text into the file representation when member nodes are absent.

\parg{File- and member-level context.} Moving from MLP to file-level dependency propagation produces mixed results. For W2V, F improves median macro-F1 and micro-F1 by 1.6 and 1.2 points, respectively, with positive differences on six systems for both metrics. For N2V, the corresponding medians decline by 2.9 and 3.2 points, with positive differences on only one and two systems. For \ft{}, the medians improve by 2.9 and 0.9 points, with positive differences on eight and seven systems, respectively. Significant gains outnumber losses for W2V and \ft{}, whereas the reverse holds for N2V. HDC is the only system that declines significantly under F for all three representations and both metrics.
 
Adding member nodes to F changes the median macro-F1 by $+1.5$ for W2V, $+0.9$ for N2V, and $+1.5$ for \ft{}, and the median micro-F1 by $+2.6$, $+2.3$, and $+1.8$. Both gains and losses are significant on several systems; for example, macro-F1 declines significantly on three systems for W2V and on two for \ft{}. Adding member nodes also changes how features enter the model, depending on the representation. 

\parg{Folder-level context.} 
Adding folder nodes produces the largest and most consistent changes. FF improves over F, and FFM improves over FM, on both metrics for every system and representation. All 120 system--representation--metric comparisons are significant with a large effect. Across the three representations, the median macro-F1 gains range from 18.1 to 32.9 points for FF $-$ F and from 17.4 to 47.2 points for FFM $-$ FM. The corresponding micro-F1 gains range from 16.7 to 33.7 points and from 16.9 to 37.6 points. Each comparison improves in at least 98 of the 100 paired runs.

Within the folder-containing configurations, adding member nodes remains beneficial but less uniform. FFM improves macro-F1 over FF on ten systems for W2V and eight systems each for N2V and \ft{}, with median gains of 11.2, 3.3, and 4.4 points. For micro-F1, it improves ten, eight, and ten systems, with median gains of 4.5, 2.5, and 2.5 points. The only significant macro-F1 loss is N2V on Bash.

\parg{Directory agreement, repairs and damage.\label{sec:directory_errors}}
The large gains from adding folder nodes show that directory organization is informative for the evaluated mappings. To examine whether these gains also help files that directory information alone does not place, we compare FM and FFM with \ft{} features against the directory hierarchy baseline of Section~\ref{sec:dir_predictor}. The baseline reaches 91--100\% accuracy on every system, and its median number of errors per split is zero on C.Img, SH-3D, and T.Mates, and at most 24 on all other systems except Lucene (78) and Chrome (549).

Without folder nodes, FM damages 17--47\% of the baseline's correct predictions on every system and remains 15--42 points below the baseline in accuracy. FFM damages at most 7\% and lies between seven points below and six points above the baseline. It exceeds the baseline only on Lucene and HDF, equals it on SH-3D, and remains below it on the other systems. Thus, folder nodes bring the model close to the information already available from the directory hierarchy.

FFM does not consistently improve the files on which the directory baseline fails. It repairs more baseline errors than FM on five systems, including Lucene (93\% versus 46\%) and HDF (64\% versus 32\%), but repairs fewer on JabRef (20\% versus 47\%) and Bash (10\% versus 17\%). JabRef shows why aggregate improvement is not sufficient: although FFM with \ft{} improves overall accuracy, it loses 49 correct FM predictions and gains nine among files misclassified by the directory baseline. Bash shows little net change on these files, with 22 predictions lost and 21 gained. These are file-split events, so the same file can contribute in several splits, and they compare FM with FFM, not each model with the baseline.

Lucene and HDF improve on the baseline's errors, with 330 and 31 gained predictions and four lost predictions each. All of the baseline's errors occur in directories without an initially labeled file. FFM assigns many of these files correctly, so it can recover assignments without labeled examples in the file's own directory. In these systems, directories are pure under the GT mapping, so this result does not show that FFM resolves files whose architectural assignment conflicts with their directory's dominant module.


\begin{GrayBox}{Answer to RQ2.}
Adding folder nodes improves macro-F1 and micro-F1 on all ten systems for all three representations, with significant large effects. File-level propagation and member nodes change scores less consistently, and member nodes also change feature construction. FFM largely matches the signal already available from the directory hierarchy, and on files where the directory baseline fails, adding folder nodes can replace correct FM predictions with incorrect FFM predictions. Strong folder-based scores therefore do not show that the model handles files for which directory location is insufficient or misleading.
\end{GrayBox}

\subsection*{RQ3. Neighbor Information across Predictors and Input File Representations\label{sec:rq3}}
RQ2 shows that adding structure can raise or lower performance depending on the representation and system, and that file-level message passing lowered the median macro-F1 of N2V. RQ3 tests whether neighbor information improves mapping and whether its effect depends on the predictor, the input vector, or the training schedule. We first test whether N2V encodes graph structure beyond node degree, then compare four ways of using neighbors with N2V, repeat the comparison with the other input vectors, compare absolute scores, and test whether self-training explains the model-dependent pattern.

Differences are paired \emph{on} minus \emph{off} changes in F1 points, and positive values favor neighbor information. For N2V, we report the median of the three per-seed medians over ten splits, with their range as a sensitivity summary and not a confidence interval. For the other vectors, we report the median over the ten splits.

\parg{Do N2V vectors encode structure beyond node degree?} N2V vectors are learned from the dependency graph, so we first check whether they carry more than each file's degree, using the rewired-graph and shuffled-vector controls on Lucene, JabRef, and Bash. With neighbor information off, real vectors exceed rewired vectors by 56, 23, and 34 macro-F1 points, respectively, and shuffled vectors by 54, 21, and 34 points. Thus, the real vectors encode dependency structure beyond each file's degree. With rewired vectors, HGT improves macro-F1 by 8.2, 8.9, and 2.7 points, respectively. These controls use HGT only and cover three systems, so they do not show whether the same holds for other predictors or throughout the benchmark.

\parg{Does the N2V effect depend on the model?} Figure~\ref{fig:rq3_n2v} shows the change in macro-F1 and micro-F1 when neighbor information is switched on for each model with N2V vectors. Points left of zero indicate that neighbor information lowered performance. The comparison shows that the negative N2V result in RQ2 is specific to HGT rather than a general consequence of adding neighbor information to N2V.

GAT provides the clearest positive result. Under self-training, neighbor information raises macro-F1 on all ten systems by 1.2 to 5.2 points, with the same sign for every seed. Under supervised-only training, it raises macro-F1 on nine systems; Bash is the only exception, with a change of $-0.2$ points. GAT is never significantly lower than its off condition. The neighbor \mlp{} likewise improves macro-F1 on nine systems under both schedules, by 0.7 to 7.8 points under self-training. Five of these self-training gains are significant. HDC is the exception for the neighbor \mlp{}, decreasing by 0.9 points under supervised-only training and by 0.3 points under self-training, with the same sign for every seed.

HGT shows the opposite pattern. With self-training, neighbor information reduces macro-F1 on all ten systems by 1.1 to 10.1 points. Under supervised-only training, it reduces macro-F1 on nine systems, improving only on T.Mates by 8.6 points. The reductions are significant on six systems under each schedule. \uhgt{} largely mitigates these losses: under self-training, changes range from $-1.1$ to $+2.0$ points, with gains on six systems, although the direction varies across seeds on six systems. Only HGT and \uhgt{} show significant reductions relative to their off conditions. Thus, the effect of neighbor information with N2V depends on how the predictor incorporates it.

\parg{Does the pattern hold for other input representations?} Unlike N2V, file-level message passing raised median macro-F1 for W2V and \ft{} in RQ2, but the on/off comparison in Table~\ref{tab:rq3_reps} shows the same split between models. HGT lowers median macro-F1 under self-training with W2V ($-4.4$ points), \zs{} ($-1.7$), and \ft{} ($-2.5$). It improves on only two of ten systems with each vector and is significantly lower on eight systems with W2V and four with \ft{}, but on only one with \zs{}. GAT and the \mlp{} instead benefit from neighbor information with all three vectors and are never significantly lower than their off condition. GAT improves macro-F1 on eight systems with W2V and on nine with \zs{} and \ft{}, and the \mlp{} on all ten with W2V and \ft{} and on nine with \zs{}. \uhgt{} improves on seven systems with W2V, nine with \zs{}, and all ten with \ft{}. The gains of GAT and the \mlp{} are largest with \zs{} ($+6.9$ and $+7.7$ points). HDC, the exception for the \mlp{} with N2V, improves with W2V, \zs{}, and \ft{} by 4.7, 8.5, and 6.8 points, respectively.

The RQ2 and RQ3 results test different comparisons. In RQ2, F is compared with the graph-free MLP, which changes the predictor together with the available structure, and the later steps change the structure alone. RQ3 holds the predictor fixed and switches dependency neighbors on and off. A gain of F over the MLP in RQ2 can therefore come from the predictor and does not imply that HGT benefits when dependency neighbors are switched on.

\parg{Can self-training explain the model-dependent effect?} Neighbor effects retain their sign across the two schedules on most systems, with T.Mates under HGT as the main exception. Self-training therefore does not explain why neighbors help GAT and the \mlp{} but lower HGT. Self-training does, however, lower macro-F1 relative to supervised-only training at the same epoch budget: with N2V on all ten systems for HGT and on eight or nine for the other models, and with W2V and \ft{} in 66 of 80 system--model combinations. This loss is accompanied by a fall in macro recall of 3 to 5 points, while micro-F1 changes by at most 1.2 points.

Figure~\ref{fig:rq3_agg} summarizes absolute supervised-only scores across the ten systems. GAT has the highest median micro-F1 and macro-F1 with every vector. Both GAT and the neighbor \mlp{} exceed the graph-free MLP in every median comparison, whereas HGT falls below it in macro-F1 with N2V and \ft{}. Across vectors, \ft{} is highest or tied, N2V is next, and W2V is lowest. Thus, the stronger scores of GAT and the neighbor \mlp{} do not eliminate the advantage of dependency-informed initializations; \ft{} with GAT gives the highest scores overall.

\begin{GrayBox}{Answer to RQ3.}
Dependency information improves mapping depending on how the predictor uses it. Neighbors improve GAT and the neighbor \mlp{} on most systems across all three representations, but HGT propagation usually lowers macro-F1. Using HGT on the untyped, undirected graph largely removes this loss, although direction and edge typing change together. GAT generally achieves the highest scores, and \ft{} is the strongest input overall. These patterns hold under both training schedules, so self-training does not explain the model-dependent neighbor effect.
\end{GrayBox}
